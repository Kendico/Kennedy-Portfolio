01

DEMONSTRATION SAMPLE

Personal brand content strategy

Seven-day LinkedIn sequence for a technology founder

DEMONSTRATION SAMPLE · Brief: Build credibility with B2B buyers, operators and early-stage founders.

DAY 1 — POSITIONING

Your product is not your positioning.

A product description tells people what you built. Positioning tells them why it matters. Strong positioning makes clear who the product is for, what problem it addresses and why this approach is worth considering. Clarity is not a branding luxury. It is a growth tool.

DAY 2 — CUSTOMER INSIGHT

Your best marketing meeting might be a customer conversation.

Ask customers what they already care about. Listen for repeated phrases, previous attempts, buying concerns and what changed after they found a solution. Research starts with asking better questions—and paying attention to the answers.

DAY 3 — EXECUTION

A busy team is not automatically a productive team.

Before adding another meeting, ask what decision must come out of it, who needs to attend and what can be handled asynchronously. Good operations protect attention.

DAY 4 — LEADERSHIP

The hardest part of delegation is letting go of your preferred method.

Define the outcome, constraints, decision-making authority and escalation point. Then leave room for someone else to think.

DAY 5 — FRAMEWORK

A simple test for every piece of business content.

Is it relevant? Is it specific? Is it useful? Does the reader leave with a clearer idea, a better question or a sensible next step? Useful consistency builds trust.

DAY 6 — FOUNDER REFLECTION

You do not need to have every answer to lead well.

A leader can admit uncertainty while staying clear about what the team is learning, which decisions are reversible and when the next update will come.

DAY 7 — CONVERSATION

What is one business process you would redesign if you could start again?

Small operational friction becomes expensive when repeated every day. What would you redesign first?

DEMONSTRATION SAMPLE

Newsletter writing

The Operator’s Note — fictional sample issue

DEMONSTRATION SAMPLE · The newsletter title and publication are fictional.

SAMPLE ISSUE

The hidden cost of making every decision yourself.

There is a point in building a business when being involved in everything stops being a sign of commitment and starts becoming a constraint. As the business grows, centralised decisions create a queue around one person. A team member waits for approval. A customer issue sits unresolved. The cost is not only the founder’s time. It is the organisation’s ability to respond.

THE DIFFERENCE

Control and clarity are not the same thing.

Control says: “Bring every decision to me.” Clarity says: “Here is the outcome we need, the boundary you must respect and the decisions you can make without me.” The second approach creates a team that can move when the founder is not in the room.

A 20-MINUTE RESET

Choose one recurring decision that regularly comes back to you.

Write down the outcome, non-negotiable limits, the person who can own the decision and the situations that genuinely require escalation. Share the framework, invite questions and review the result—not every movement along the way.

CLOSING

A business should not become less capable every time its founder becomes unavailable.

The aim is not to make the founder irrelevant. It is to make the organisation more capable. What is one decision you could stop being the default owner of?

DEMONSTRATION SAMPLE

Thought leadership article

AI will not replace good thinking. It will expose the absence of it.

DEMONSTRATION SAMPLE · Original opinion article for a business and technology audience.

OPENING

Output is not the same thing as judgement.

The easiest way to misunderstand artificial intelligence is to treat the speed of its output as proof of the quality of its thinking. AI can produce a first draft in seconds. That speed is useful. It is also capable of hiding a serious weakness: output is not the same thing as judgement.

THE NEW BOTTLENECK

The new bottleneck is not production.

As production becomes easier, deciding what deserves to be produced becomes more valuable. Which question matters? Which source is reliable? What is missing from the brief? These are questions of judgement.

FLUENCY AND FACTS

Fluent answers can still be wrong.

Fluency does not guarantee accuracy, relevance or sound reasoning. Verify important claims, test assumptions and keep a person accountable for the final decision. Do not confuse a plausible answer with a dependable one.

BETTER QUESTIONS

The people who benefit most will ask better questions.

Using AI well begins with understanding the task well enough to describe the outcome, audience, constraints and standard of success. A thoughtful brief gives the human reviewer a basis for judging the result.

PRACTICAL CLOSE

AI can accelerate the work. Good thinking still has to direct it.

Use AI to generate options, organise notes and identify counterarguments. Then check the facts, remove what does not fit, add context and decide whether the recommendation is appropriate for the people affected.
